When a user reports a suspicious email, the first things they notice are usually the sender's display name, the subject line, or an urgent request inside the message body. An email might claim to come from "IT Support Desk" with an address like `support@company.com`. However, email protocols designed in the early days of the internet lack built-in identity verification. The sender address shown in your inbox can be easily forged, a technique known as **email spoofing**.

To determine where an email actually came from and whether it can be trusted, security analysts look at the raw **email header**. Email headers contain technical tracking metadata added by every email server that handled the message on its journey from sender to recipient.

## What is an Email Header?

Think of an email like a physical letter:

- The **body** is the message inside the envelope, including any text, images, or attachments.
- The **header** is like the stamps, postmarks, and routing tags stamped on the outside of the envelope by every post office that handled it.

Standard email programs like Outlook, Apple Mail, or Gmail hide raw headers to keep the interface clean. However, every email client allows you to view the raw headers or message source. 

While the email body tells you what the sender wants you to read, the email header tells you how the email arrived at your system.

## Key Components of an Email Header

Email headers consist of key-value pairs separated by colons. Below are the core fields analysts look at during an investigation.

### 1. `From:`
This is the display address that the recipient sees in their inbox interface. It is set by the sender's email program and can be forged easily without technical effort.

### 2. `Return-Path:`
Also known as the envelope sender address. This field specifies where bounce notifications or delivery error reports should be sent if the email fails to reach the recipient. Attackers often forge the display `From:` address to mimic a trusted brand while leaving their actual sending domain in the `Return-Path:`.

### 3. `Reply-To:`
This defines the email address that will populate if the recipient clicks the "Reply" button. If the `From:` address says `hr-desk@yourcompany.com` but the `Reply-To:` address points to `attacker@external-domain.com`, responses will go straight to the attacker.

### 4. `Message-ID:`
A unique identifier generated by the mail server that first processed the message (e.g., `<202611160912.abc12345@mail.sender.com>`). Discrepancies between the domain in the `Message-ID` and the domain in the `From:` address can point to spoofing.

### 5. `Received:` Lines
`Received:` headers document every server (known as a Mail Transfer Agent or MTA) that processed and forwarded the email. 

Each server adds a new `Received:` line to the **top** of the existing headers as it receives the email. Because of this stacking order, you must read `Received:` headers from **bottom to top** to follow the chronological path of the email from origin to destination.

## Email Authentication Frameworks: SPF, DKIM, and DMARC

To protect against spoofing, receiving mail servers use three primary authentication standards. The results of these checks are recorded directly in the header under the `Authentication-Results:` line.

### SPF (Sender Policy Framework)
SPF allows a domain owner to publish a list of IP addresses authorized to send email on behalf of their domain using a special DNS record. 

When your email server receives a message, it checks the IP address of the sending server against the sender domain's SPF record.
- **Pass:** The sending IP address is authorized.
- **Fail / Softfail:** The sending IP address is not listed as an authorized sender.

### DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail)
DKIM adds a digital cryptographic signature to emails. The sender's server signs parts of the header and body using a private key. The receiving server uses the sender's public key (published in DNS) to verify the signature.
- **Pass:** The signature is valid, confirming the message was not modified in transit and came from the claimed domain.
- **Fail:** The signature is missing, invalid, or altered during transit.

### DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance)
DMARC ties SPF and DKIM together. It requires **alignment**, meaning the domain in the visible `From:` header must match the domain authenticated by SPF and/or DKIM.

DMARC also tells receiving mail servers what action to take if authentication fails:
- `p=none`: Monitor and log, but allow delivery.
- `p=quarantine`: Send failed emails to the spam/junk folder.
- `p=reject`: Block failed emails entirely.

## Walkthrough: Analyzing a Suspicious Header

Below is an annotated snippet of a raw header from a phishing email attempting to impersonate a financial service.

```text
Delivered-To: victim@company.com
Received: by 10.0.0.5 with SMTP id a123; Mon, 16 Nov 2026 09:15:02 -0500
Received: from mail.suspicious-relay.net (mail.suspicious-relay.net [198.51.100.45])
    by mx.company.com with ESMTP id b456
    for <victim@company.com>; Mon, 16 Nov 2026 09:15:01 -0500
Authentication-Results: mx.company.com;
    dkim=fail header.i=@legitbank.com;
    spf=softfail (mx.company.com: domain of transition status line indicates 198.51.100.45 is not allowed) smtp.mailfrom=bounce@suspicious-relay.net;
    dmarc=fail (p=QUARANTINE) action=quarantine header.from=legitbank.com
Return-Path: <bounce@suspicious-relay.net>
Reply-To: <verify-account@attacker-control.com>
From: "Legit Bank Security" <no-reply@legitbank.com>
To: <victim@company.com>
Subject: Urgent: Verify Your Account Credentials
Date: Mon, 16 Nov 2026 14:14:55 +0000
Message-ID: <20261116141455.78910@suspicious-relay.net>
```

### Breaking Down the Telemetry

1. **Compare Address Fields:**
   - `From:` claims the email is from `no-reply@legitbank.com`.
   - `Return-Path:` shows `bounce@suspicious-relay.net`.
   - `Reply-To:` points to `verify-account@attacker-control.com`.
   
   *Analysis:* The mismatch between the display address (`legitbank.com`) and the routing/reply addresses (`suspicious-relay.net` and `attacker-control.com`) strongly indicates email spoofing.

2. **Trace the Path (`Received:` lines):**
   - Reading from bottom to top, the message originated from server `mail.suspicious-relay.net` at IP address `198.51.100.45`.
   - It was directly delivered to the target organization's mail server (`mx.company.com`).

3. **Check Authentication Results:**
   - **SPF:** `softfail`. IP address `198.51.100.45` is not an authorized sender for `legitbank.com`.
   - **DKIM:** `fail`. The cryptographic signature for `@legitbank.com` failed verification.
   - **DMARC:** `fail`. Because SPF and DKIM both failed alignment with the domain in the `From:` header (`legitbank.com`), DMARC failed and triggered the receiver's quarantine action.

## Practical Investigation Steps for Analysts

When analyzing a reported suspicious email, follow a structured header analysis process:

1. **Extract the Raw Source:** Obtain the original, un-forwarded email message file (`.eml` or `.msg`) to preserve raw headers. Forwarding an email modifies the header structure.
2. **Verify Header Alignment:** Check if the domain in the `From:` header matches the domains present in `Return-Path:`, `Reply-To:`, and `Message-ID:`.
3. **Inspect the Originating IP:** Locate the bottom-most `Received:` line to identify the initial sending server's IP address. Look up the IP in reputation databases or WHOIS records to determine its location and ownership.
4. **Evaluate Authentication Results:** Review the `Authentication-Results:` line to verify SPF, DKIM, and DMARC status.
5. **Cross-Reference Links and Attachments:** Use the findings from the header investigation to contextualize links or file attachments found in the body of the message.

## Limitations and Edge Cases

Header analysis provides clear technical evidence, but context is essential due to common edge cases:

- **Email Forwarding:** Automatic forwarding rules often break SPF checks because the forwarding server passes the email along from a new IP address not listed in the original sender's SPF record. However, DKIM signatures usually survive forwarding intact.
- **Third-Party Email Platforms:** Organizations often hire third-party services (like marketing tools or payroll systems) to send emails on their behalf. If the organization forgets to update their SPF record with the vendor's IP addresses, legitimate internal communications may trigger SPF softfails.
- **DMARC `p=none` Policies:** Many legitimate domains deploy DMARC in monitoring mode (`p=none`). When an email fails DMARC under a `p=none` policy, receiving systems will log the failure but still deliver the email to the user's inbox unless secondary spam controls flag it.

## Conclusion

Display names and visible sender fields in email clients can easily deceive users, but email headers record objective technical details about where a message originated and how it moved across the internet. By verifying header fields, tracing `Received:` hops, and interpreting SPF, DKIM, and DMARC results, security analysts can reliably spot spoofed messages and make informed triage decisions.
